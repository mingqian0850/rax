4 The RAX System RAX supports a wide range of embodiments in simulation and in the real world, including
single-arm and bimanual manipulators, dexterous hands, mobile manipulators, and humanoids, with
one agent architecture. To do so, it adopts a separation familiar from software engineering, in
which a frontend is kept apart from the backend that serves it. In RAX, the backend is the robot’s physical stack: the
hardware drivers or the simulator, the low-level controllers, the sensor pipelines, and the
safeguards around them. The frontend is the agent’s workspace, where the agent observes,
decides, and acts, and where it writes the programs that encode its decision logic. The two meet
only at a tool interface ( Figure 4 ), and the same frontend connects to the backend of every platform, from
the real Franka and Rainbow RB-Y1 robots to each simulated benchmark. The agent never touches the
backend: it cannot modify controllers, drivers, or robot infrastructure. Because the agent acts only through these tools, its programs contain the
decision logic of a task, namely perception, planning, and the checks between stages, while
low-level control, from joint-level PD control to whole-body motion tracking on humanoids, and
the enforcement of safety limits remain in the backend. The remainder of this
section describes the separation and what each side provides ( Section 4.1 ), and the
control interface through which one frontend drives arms, dexterous hands, mobile manipulators,
and humanoids ( Section 4.2 ). 4.1 Frontend–Backend Separation Figure 4 : Frontend–backend separation. The agent and its programs act only through the
tool interface; the backend owns control, sensing, and safeguards on every platform. On a real robot, the backend owns everything that keeps the hardware calibrated and safe. It runs
the vendor’s drivers and real-time controllers, serves calibrated RGB-D images together with the
intrinsics and poses of their cameras, and records the robot’s sensors and state continuously,
so that the agent can review the outcome of an action after the fact. It also enforces the
safeguards. Motion is enabled only while an operator has armed the robot, and disarming halts it
immediately; every motion command is validated before execution against workspace bounds, joint
limits, and self-collision, and on the RB-Y1 mobile manipulator also against joint load. A command that violates
a limit is refused before the robot moves, together with the limit it violated, so that the agent
can change its approach. The frontend receives exactly two things from the system, tools and skills. The tools expose as much of the backend as
possible: observation, raw control, and model-based helpers in the form of forward and inverse
kinematics, motion planning where the platform offers it, and a preview that executes a candidate
motion on the robot model alone, without the scene. They also provide the geometric primitives on
which perception is built, namely the back-projection of pixels to metric 3D points, the poses of
the cameras in the robot’s frame, and the kinematic chain from each camera to the end-effectors.
The skills are documents that describe how to work with these tools: the robot’s frames,
action spaces, rates, limits, and failure modes, the conventions of perception, and the workflow
of exploration and memory. They are organized along four axes, namely task strategy,
coordination, perception, and robot-specific control, and are composed into a single workspace
for each session according to its embodiment and observation mode. Everything that solves a task,
from object models and perception pipelines to grasps and control code, is written by the agent,
which works in an isolated container with a persistent workspace and standard programming tools. Figure 5 : A long-horizon task on the real RB-Y1. The agent composes six sub-tasks from skills
learned in earlier sessions. It finds the cups from Gemini’s rim points and depth, measures the desk
edge by picking points in the head image, and plans the base routes on a model of the scene. This division has three consequences. First, programs written by the agent call exactly
the tools that the agent itself calls, so code developed interactively runs unchanged as
a fully automated program. Second, the interface returns compact results, consisting of
structured state and the final image of each motion, while the complete images and actions of
every motion are stored as episodic evidence that the agent retrieves on demand, which keeps the
agent’s context small over sessions that last many hours. Third, on real robots, the skills that
agents verify are kept in a versioned repository for each robot, in which every session works on
its own branch, and a contribution is merged only after its structure, its entry point, and the
evidence of its unattended success have been validated. Most importantly, the separation makes RAX multi-embodiment by construction. Bringing up a new
robot requires only a backend that exposes it through the tool interface, together with documentation of its control and
sensing interfaces, while the frontend, the organization of
memory, and the evaluation protocol are
shared across all platforms. No expert teleoperation data is collected and no policy is trained to bring a robot into
RAX. The single-arm, bimanual, dexterous, mobile, and humanoid embodiments of our experiments,
in simulation and in the real world, differ only in their backends and in the
documentation of their interfaces, and the agent learns to control each of them through exploration. 4.2 Multi-Embodiment Control Interface RAX adopts a layered control design. Low-level controllers, from joint position and impedance controllers to whole-body motion
trackers, run in the backend and are responsible for high-rate tracking and
stability, while the agent makes decisions at the level of short action chunks and motion goals.
Each platform exposes raw control for full expressiveness, together with model-based helpers in
the form of forward and inverse kinematics, collision-aware motion
planning ( Sundaralingam et al., 2023 ) , and a robot-only preview that executes a candidate
motion on the robot model without the scene. The agent thus chooses its own level of
abstraction, from individual joint targets to planned free-space motions. Table 1 summarizes this construction for each platform. Table 1: Low-level control on each platform. EEF: end effector; OSC: operational-space

moves of the tool center point, joint-space moves whose paths are checked against the workspace
and the table before the arm moves, and force-controlled grasps that close the gripper to a
target width with a set force. The Rainbow RB-Y1, a wheeled mobile manipulator with a torso, two arms, and
a two-joint head that carries an RGB-D camera, runs joint position control on its robot controller,
which also accepts Cartesian IK commands for the torso and arms. The agent commands end-effector
poses, joint-space postures of the torso, arms, and head, coordinated motions of the torso and arms, motions
of the differential-drive base, and the gaze of the head camera. It can query the vendor’s
kinematic model for the collisions, joint load, and center of mass of any posture, and dry-run any
motion command to learn whether it would be accepted. Neither robot offers a motion planner, so
the agent composes long motions from short checked moves through clear intermediate postures. On the HumanoidArena
benchmark ( Wang et al., 2026b ) , the agent commands the Unitree G1 through whole-body
kinematic references rather than joint torques: each reference specifies a desired heading, a
full joint configuration, and hand commands, and a whole-body motion tracker converts these references into balanced whole-body motion. Because the robot moves through space only by
stepping, locomotion is not provided as a primitive but must be constructed by the agent from leg
references, and agents do so by measuring their own gait, calibrating step length, and composing
walking, turning, and stepping over obstacles from these measurements. On DexJoCo ( Wang et al., 2026a ) , the official
operational-space controller tracks the flange pose of each arm while position actuators drive
the sixteen joints of each Allegro hand, and no motion planner is
provided, so the agent plans the motion of each arm and the configuration of each hand itself. The remaining simulated
platforms span the same range of abstraction. The single arm of LIBERO and
LIBERO-Pro ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2025 ) is driven through the benchmark’s own
operational-space controller by relative end-effector commands alone, whereas on
RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026a ) and BEHAVIOR-1K ( Li et al., 2023 ) the native controllers
execute joint targets under each benchmark’s own dynamics, and inverse kinematics and motion
planning are offered as helpers alongside raw control rather than as replacements for it. Motion
planning accounts for the robot and static structures but never for task objects, which the agent
must perceive itself.
