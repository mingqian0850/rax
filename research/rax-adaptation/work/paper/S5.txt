5 Experiments In this section we report our simulation benchmark results as well as case studies on both simulation and real robot experiments. 5.1 Setup We comprehensively evaluate RAX in both simulated environments and on real-world robotic hardware. In simulation, the evaluation spans six benchmarks covering single-arm, bimanual, dexterous, mobile, and humanoid embodiments: RoboDojo, BEHAVIOR-1K, HumanoidArena, DexJoCo, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Pro. In each session, a coding agent starts in a localized workspace containing only the platform’s baseline skills. Guided by a task description and RGB-D observations, the agent explores training scenes within a strictly defined episode budget. The submitted code is then executed as a fully automated program under each benchmark’s official evaluation protocol ( Section 10 ). On physical robots, RAX is deployed on a Franka FR3 manipulator to master five distinct tabletop tasks: (1) memory , tracking and finding a hidden cube randomly shuffled under four cups ( Section 14.1 ); (2) articulated objects , opening an air fryer drawer, inserting a banana, and closing it ( Section 14.2 ); (3) deformable objects , wiping a stain off a table using a wet towel ( Section 14.3 ); (4) precision , inserting pegs into a ManipulationNet board with a stringent 0.1,mm clearance ( Section 14.4 ); and (5) precision and long horizon , dismantling and rebuilding a Bandu tower of irregular pieces ( Section 14.5 ). Additionally, we evaluate RAX on a Rainbow RB-Y1 mobile manipulator to solve a long-horizon logistical task. This involves pulling out a chair, stacking cups, picking up a banana, loading the items into a basket, carrying the basket to a cabinet, tucking the chair back, and subsequently transferring a box to the cabinet ( Section 14.6 ). Crucially, the agent receives only natural-language instructions and zero teleoperated demonstrations. It is designed to autonomously evaluate its own success criteria, only prompting a human operator to reset the physical scene between trials. 5.2 Simulation Results Here we report the simulation benchmark results across RoboDojo, BEHAVIOR-1K, HumanoidArena, DexJoCo, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Pro. 5.2.1 RoboDojo Setup. For each task, a single agent explores a single environment from an empty workspace, given the task’s public scoring rubric with a short clarification of its conditions, RGB-D observations, and one image of the task’s completed state. To understand where this gap comes from, we inspect the fully automated programs submitted
by the RGB-D and RGB-only agents on make toast random with a budget of 32 episodes. Both agents use the
vision-language model in the same role, to point at semantic image locations such as the
toaster slots, the top crust of a bread slice, and the lever. They differ in how they turn
these image points into motion. With depth. The RGB-D agent lifts every image point into a metric 3D point and
builds the task’s geometry from these points. It first refines each slot opening by
fitting a line to its visible contour, then measures both rim endpoints of each slot in 3D,
from which it derives the slot centers and their common heading. On the bread, it measures
two points along the top crust and one on the near side, and recovers the slice’s full 3D
orientation from the plane through these points. After the grasp, it measures the slice
again in the wrist camera to obtain its pose in the hand, and repeats this measurement above
the slot to correct any slip before release. The agent also uses metric geometry to verify
its perception: a measurement is rejected when a rim point does not lie within a narrow band
at the toaster’s roof height, when the two rim endpoints are not 9 to 20 cm apart, or when
the two slots are not 2.6 to 7.5 cm apart, and a press of the lever counts only when the
lever is measured to have travelled 2.5 to 13 cm downward. Without depth. The RGB-only agent does not give up on 3D reasoning; it
reconstructs a coarse camera model of its own. It maps overhead-camera pixels to table
coordinates through a linear calibration at an assumed height, about 1.8 mm per pixel
across the image and 1.4 mm per pixel along it, and then refines every target by visual
servoing in the wrist camera: it moves the gripper until the target is centered, with
hand-tuned gains of 0.4 to 0.8 mm per pixel, and rotates it until the slot or the bread is
aligned in the image. The quantities that depth would provide are replaced by constants,
including the grasp and release heights, the 3.8 cm spacing between the slots, and the
height to which the lever is pressed, and success is judged by asking the vision-language
model whether a slice is held or seated. These approximations hold only where the scene
matches the agent’s assumptions. In evaluation, 36 of its 45 episodes ended in the lever
stage without success, the step at which a fixed press height replaces a measured one,
against 8 successes for the RGB-D agent. 5.3.3 Inference Time and Tokens Here we demonstrate the decrease of inference wall time and output tokens with exploration as more and more decisions are being automated with programs. Simulation As the agent compiles its experience into autonomous code programs, the computational overhead of action generation decreases substantially. We evaluate this efficiency gain across two benchmark tasks: loading the car in BEHAVIOR-1K (128 exploration episodes across two parallel environments) and make toast random in RoboDojo (64 exploration episodes). We define episode inference time as the cumulative wall-clock latency dedicated to action generation—excluding simulator and hardware step times—regardless of whether actions stem from direct LLM deliberation, code program execution, or a hybrid mode. Figure 7 tracks inference latency and output token consumption across all successful exploration episodes. On loading the car , the initial successful episode required 123 minutes of inference time and 254k output tokens. By the final exploration episode, inference overhead dropped to 4 minutes and 4k tokens, while environment execution time remained over 1 hour per episode. Similarly, on make toast random , initial task completion required 17 minutes of inference latency. Once execution transitioned to the compiled program, inference latency for the final agent-monitored successes fell to 6 seconds per episode—matching the 3–7 seconds observed during standalone program rehearsals (which accounted for 7 of the 15 total successes). that its later programs follow ( Figure 9 ). On the RB-Y1, an in-place turn that the agent

In initial sessions, the agent independently learned to park and tuck an office chair, stack nested cups, transport a bucket by its rim onto a cabinet, and pick and place a box. Each skill was committed to the repository only after achieving successful end-to-end execution, stored as a versioned program bound to explicit precondition checks. The multi-stage tidy-up task ( Figure 5 ) evaluates the composition of these skills under a single high-level goal: park the chair, stack the cups, place the cups and a banana inside the bucket, transport the bucket onto the cabinet, tuck the chair back under the desk, and finally pick the box onto the other empty cabinet. Skills are called, not relearned In the final execution of the tidy-up task, every stage is resolved by composing stored skills from the repository ( Figure 11 ). The agent executes the chair skill sequentially: it estimates the chair geometry, plans a collision-free motion path, grasps the backrest, navigates along the path, and releases the target. It invokes the cup-stacking skill using semantic visual grounding from the current scene, applying fine-grained closed-loop offsets prior to insertion. For the bucket and box skills, the agent parameterizes execution using real-time perception data, such as the estimated cabinet boundary derived from depth sensing. To bridge gaps between stored skills, the agent dynamically synthesizes ad-hoc code—such as extending the grasp planner to handle a banana, commanding torso elevation prior to transit, and re-estimating desk pose before final chair insertion. Skills are refined in use Across three iterations of the tidy-up task, the agent iteratively updated program versions rather than rewriting entire control loops. The bucket and box skills required minor programmatic adjustments ( 25 out of more than 1200 lines) to integrate dynamic cabinet-edge measurements from onboard depth perception. Skills encountering greater environmental domain shift underwent larger revisions: following two failed grasp attempts, the agent updated the chair skill to grasp 4 cm deeper, while extending the cup-stacking skill to support picking additional object classes. For instance, a synthesized hand-reach primitive was automatically abstracted, committed to the repository, and subsequently reused across both the chair-manipulation and cup-stacking routines. 5.5.2 Rapid Adaptation of Programs When task conditions vary post-deployment, RAX preserves valid program subroutines and selectively updates only the modules invalidated by environmental shifts ( Figure 12 ). On the Franka manipulator, two end-to-end autonomous programs were adapted to novel task variants within the same session. Find the cube In a hidden-object task, the physical occlusion sequence was modified: rather than directly inverting a cup over the target cube, the cube was placed inside an upright cup prior to inversion. The original program detected no covering cup and safely halted execution via exception handling without initiating ungrounded motion. The agent extended its perception pipeline with a detector for cubes contained within upright cups, validated the new logic against current and historical sensor logs, and resumed execution to complete the task ( Figure 22 ). Crucially, the cup-tracking, execution-monitoring, and removal routines were reused without modification. When substituting the cube with a red wedge, the agent generalized its geometric object representation from a fixed cube primitive to an object parameterized by estimated dimensions, color, and height, adjusting grasp synthesis accordingly while preserving 20 of the original 41 program functions (including the tracking pipeline; Figure 23 ). Wiping with paper towels Following the acquisition of a reliable surface-wiping skill using a wet cloth, the task was extended to require pulling disposable paper towels from a dispenser box, executing the wiping motion, and discarding used towels into a receptacle. The agent synthesized a composite program that imported the original wiping module as a dependency. The new program reused existing subroutines for stain segmentation against the table background, reachable workspace estimation, pressing by position control ( below the measured surface contact, along a serpentine trajectory), and post-wipe residue verification. Only paper-handling subroutines were added: locating the dispenser box and disposal bin, extracting individual sheets onto the stain, discarding the crumpled towel, and clearing loose paper from the workspace. The stain was removed in both wet runs, although leftover sheets still had to be cleared with supervised scripts ( Section 14.3 , Figure 29 ).
