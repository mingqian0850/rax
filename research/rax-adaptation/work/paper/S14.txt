sensors, what the agent was given, what the operator did, how many trials the agent ran, how
success was judged, and the final outcome. Five tasks use a Franka FR3 arm with a parallel
gripper, two exterior RGB-D cameras (left and right of the table), and an RGB-D camera on the

no object models, and no trained policy. Each session ran Claude Code with maximum reasoning
effort. The agent could send requests to the operator through its tools, and the operator could

local wall-clock times taken from the agent’s notes and the camera recordings, which are timestamped

drawer of an air fryer by its handle, put a banana into the basket, and close the drawer, on a
real Franka FR3 with a left exterior and a wrist RGB-D camera. The agent wrote the perception
and the program itself, in about 1,500 lines of Python; they use no learned detector and no
vision-language model. All numbers below are from the agent’s final run. Lifting to a metric frame. Every depth pixel is back-projected with the camera
intrinsics and the camera-to-base pose into the robot’s base frame, and the views of the
exterior and wrist cameras are fused in that frame. All later computations operate on these
points; estimates are projected back into the images only to check them. Segmentation and geometric primitives. Each part of the scene is selected by a
colour threshold combined with a height band and a workspace region: the dark lid on top of the
fryer, its white body, the dark handle outside the body, the yellow banana, and an orange ball
just above the table. Geometric primitives are then fitted to the selected points, each with a
plausibility check on its size: • the fryer body, by a robust circle fit to its side (radius 8 to 16 cm accepted), and
the lid, seen from the wrist camera above, by a second circle (4.5 to 8 cm), whose centre
gives the fryer’s vertical axis ; • the handle, by the direction from the axis to its horizontal arm, which gives the pull
direction , the outer face of its grip in 1.5 cm height slices, the height of its arm,
and its lateral offset; • the banana, by its principal axis and a quadratic centre line, which give its arc
middle, the normal on its convex side, its chord (13 to 21 cm accepted; 18.0 cm here), and
its height; • the basket, once the drawer is open, by a circle fitted to its rim. The pull direction from the top view must agree with the one from the side view within
12 ∘ ; the side view alone was biased by about , which is why the agent added the
top view. Task frame and articulated model. All targets are expressed in a frame anchored on
the fryer: the axis , the pull direction , and . The agent also wrote an
articulated model of the fryer: a fixed upper cylinder and the back of the lower body, and a
drawer cylinder with box models of the handle’s grip and arm, which translate together by the
drawer’s opening along . The model provides a signed distance from any point, and the
program instantiates it at the drawer’s current opening to check every planned motion for
clearance between the arm, hand, fingers, wrist camera, and held object, and the fryer, the
table, and keep-out regions around the exterior cameras. How the agent built the model. No model of the fryer was provided. The agent first
completed the task once by hand, controlling the robot step by step, and recorded what it measured
in its notes: the fryer’s top at a height of 0.415 m and the seam between body and drawer at
0.245 m, a handle with a horizontal arm and a hanging grip, and the basket centre relative to the
handle once the drawer was out. It also noted that the drawer slides freely along the pull
direction and tips slightly when released. About 90 minutes into the session it turned these
observations into the model above, in the first version of its program, and revised it in later
versions. Table 21 separates what the agent estimates in each new scene from what
