10 Benchmarks and Tasks This section gives the protocol that all simulation experiments share and then the setting of
each benchmark and its tasks. The real-robot tasks are described in Section 14 . 10.1 Sessions Agents. Every experiment is a session of one coding agent in its own command-line
harness: Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 or, on four RoboDojo tasks and one BEHAVIOR-1K task,
Codex with a GPT-6 model, at the xhigh reasoning effort unless stated otherwise. A session
provides one or more simulated environments; with more than one, the agent may start subagents
of the same model, which share its workspace and its budget. The weights of the model are never
updated. Workspace. The agent starts from an empty workspace. Its folders for code, notes, and
outputs are empty, and it holds only the instructions of the harness, the task description, the
contract of the tool interface, and the skills of the platform, composed for the embodiment and
the observation mode of the session ( Section 9 ); the agent keeps no memory from earlier
sessions. It runs in a container without network access, with 16 GB of memory and four CPUs,
and reaches the simulator only through its tools. Every program it registers runs in a fresh
container with 8 GB of memory, four CPUs, and no network access. Task information. The task description is generated from the public definition of the
task; its content differs between benchmarks and is given below. Images of the completed task
are frames of successful official demonstrations, either the final frame of each or a sequence of
keyframes along each. They contain no actions and no states, and only the exploring agent sees
them, never its programs. During an interactive episode, the agent may ask the benchmark’s
evaluator whether the task is complete. Budget and scenes. The exploration budget counts exploration episodes. A single ledger
charges every episode start and every rehearsal, across all environments and subagents, and the
agent sees how many episodes remain. The budget is an upper bound, since submission ends
exploration. Exploration scenes come in a fixed order, which the agent cannot choose, from the
training distribution of the benchmark, and evaluation scenes from its official evaluation set,
which exploration never uses. Limits. Each episode has the native step limit of the benchmark and a wall-clock limit,
which during an interactive episode also bounds the agent’s own deliberation. The agent and its
programs may call a vision-language model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, with a budget of $10 per session
that exploration and evaluation share. 10.2 Rehearsal and Evaluation Rehearsal. When the agent considers its program ready, it freezes a copy of its
code with a registered entry point into a bundle and requests a rehearsal. The bundle is executed
alone in a fresh isolated environment on a new exploration scene while the agent is paused, and the
rehearsal consumes one exploration episode, like an episode in which the agent acts itself.
Afterwards, the agent reads the outcome and the logs of the rehearsal and may revise the bundle
and rehearse again. Only a bundle that has completed a rehearsal without error can be submitted,
and submission ends exploration. Fully automated program. Evaluation executes the submitted bundle on each unseen
scene in a fresh isolated environment, without the agent and without edits or retries. The
results are never revealed to the agent. Because the bundle calls the same tools that the agent
called during exploration, the program that is evaluated is the program that was rehearsed. Agent-in-the-loop program. At the end of exploration, the workspace is frozen,
including the code, the notes, the handoff, and a compressed version of the conversation. For
each evaluation scene, a fresh copy of the agent starts from this frozen workspace and attempts
the scene, running its code, observing, and acting through the same tools as during exploration. Scoring. Both forms are scored by the benchmark’s native evaluator, with partial
credit wherever the benchmark defines it. An error, a timeout, or an early exit of the program
counts as a failure, and scores are averaged over all scheduled evaluation episodes. 10.3 RoboDojo Tasks and interface. We use seven tasks of RoboDojo-Sim ( Chen et al., 2026a ) with the
dual-arm ARX X5 ( Table 13 ). The agent commands absolute joint targets at 25 Hz,
six arm joints and a gripper opening for each arm, with between 1 and 50 rows per call. It may
also command bounded end-effector steps, plan collision-free motions of one arm against the robot
and the table, lift pixels to 3D points, and preview a motion on the robot alone. It observes the
scene through a high camera and a camera on each wrist at 640 480; the benchmark renders
RGB, and the harness adds metric depth, the intrinsics, and the pose of every camera. Task information. The task description is the public scoring rubric of the task, which
for the randomized variants is that of the base task, with its score table. For make kong, pour
by language, and swap blocks it adds a short clarification of the conditions, and for plug in
charger and pour by language a summary of the official task code without its numeric thresholds.
The agent also receives one image of the completed task, the last frame of the official video of
the task. Exploration and evaluation. A single agent explores a single environment; on plug in
charger and pour by language it may start one subagent. Exploration scenes are the layouts of one
official collection in order, which repeat once the collection is exhausted, and the program is
evaluated on the first layouts of another collection ( Table 13 ); on hang mugs
random, both come from a pooled collection, with evaluation on layouts 0 to 49 and exploration on
layouts 50 to 89. An episode is limited to 6 s of wall-clock time per allowed step. A score is
100 on native success and the native partial score otherwise. The parallel experiment
( Table 7 ) uses make toast with a description that includes the numeric thresholds,
keyframes of a completed run, and 32 evaluation layouts from a third collection; the RGB-only
sessions of Table 8 receive no depth, calibration, or pixel lifting. Table 13: RoboDojo tasks. Step limit at 25 Hz; partial credit: native score

We use six tasks of the 2025 BEHAVIOR
Challenge ( Li et al., 2023 ) with the Galaxea R1Pro, in the official evaluation environment
of BEHAVIOR-1K ( Table 14 ). The agent commands the native action at 30 Hz, base
velocity together with absolute joint targets of the four trunk joints and the two seven-joint
arms and a command for each gripper, with between 1 and 150 rows per call. It may also move the
base in closed loop, step an end effector through inverse kinematics, plan collision-free arm
motions, lift pixels to 3D points, and preview a motion on the robot alone. It observes the scene
through the head camera at 720 720 and a camera on each wrist at 480 480, all RGB-D
with intrinsics and poses relative to the base, together with the official proprioception and an
odometry estimate integrated from the base velocity. The step limit of an episode is 1.5 times the
mean length of the human demonstrations of the task, as in the 2026 evaluation, whereas the 2025
challenge allowed twice the mean. Task information. The task description contains the official instruction, the goal and
initial conditions of the task’s BDDL definition, the scene and its rooms, and the limits of an
episode. Except on boxing books, the agent also receives keyframes of successful official
demonstrations from the head camera: the first frame, the end of each annotated skill, the final
frame, and enough frames in between that no gap exceeds 10 s. Exploration and evaluation. Exploration scenes are the 300 training instances of each task,
and evaluation uses the test instances 301 to 310 ( Table 14 ). An episode is
limited to 900 s plus 0.5 s per allowed step. Boxing books is evaluated as a fully automated
program and the other five tasks as agent-in-the-loop programs, with one fresh copy of the agent
per test instance. On sorting items, clearing food, and clean up desk, each copy also receives the
reports that a review model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, wrote on the fully automated program’s runs on the
other nine test instances, built from the final status of each goal condition, the partial score,
the program’s log, and camera frames, and referring to objects only by their task names. Episodes
are scored by the Q-score of the challenge: 1 on success and otherwise the fraction of goal
conditions that become satisfied during the episode, maximized over the alternative groundings of

We use the seven tasks of HumanoidArena ( Wang et al., 2026b ) that have official test episodes, with a Unitree G1 that has 29 body joints and Dex3 hands
( Table 15 ). The agent commands the official 40-dimensional whole-body
reference at 50 Hz, with between 1 and 100 frames per call: the root translation and height,
which the SONIC tracker ( Luo et al., 2026 ) does not follow, since the robot translates only by
stepping, the root orientation relative to the initial heading, 29 joint targets, and an open or
close command for each hand. It may also plan collision-free motions of the arms, optionally with
the waist, and lift pixels to 3D points in the pelvis frame and in a gravity-aligned frame. It
observes the scene through the head camera at 640 480 in RGB-D, with the intrinsics and the
camera pose in the pelvis frame, together with the root orientation, the joint positions and
velocities, and the state of the hands. An episode ends when the robot falls, by the rule of the
benchmark. Task information. The agent can read three teleoperated demonstrations of the task
from the official raw dataset. Each is a complete episode with the 40-dimensional
references, joint states, root poses, and the head camera’s RGB video and depth. The
demonstrations are mounted read-only for the exploring agent only; its programs may contain
motion segments that the agent cut from them ( Section 11.3 ). Exploration and evaluation. We use Claude Opus 5.5 as our agent. Exploration scenes use seeds derived from
a hash that excludes every official seed. Sessions with a budget above 32 explore two environments
and may start two subagents. An episode is limited to 600 s plus 1 s per allowed step. The
program is evaluated on the 60 official test episodes of each task, seeds 0, 1, and 2 with 20
episodes each, derived as in the official evaluation; every episode runs in a fresh simulator and
container, four in parallel, and the simulator advances only when the program sends actions.

We use the eleven tasks of DexJoCo ( Wang et al., 2026a ) in its
rand-obj setting, in which the placement of the objects and the height of the table are
randomized at every reset ( Table 16 ). Single-arm tasks use one Franka Panda arm
with a 16-degree-of-freedom Allegro hand, and bimanual tasks use two. The agent commands the
official policy action of the benchmark: for each arm, an absolute target for the flange
position and orientation in the world frame, the orientation as a rotation vector, together with
sixteen hand joint targets, which gives a 22-dimensional row for one arm and a 44-dimensional
row for two. The official operational-space controller pulls the flange toward its target and
position actuators drive the hand joints, without a motion planner. Each row advances the
simulation by 20 ms, and one call to the interface executes between 1 and 150 rows.

in millimeters and the intrinsics and world pose of every camera, together with the official
proprioceptive state, the flange pose of each arm and the joint positions of each hand. No
force, torque, contact, or tactile signal is exposed, and the object poses and table height in
the simulator’s state never reach the agent. The interface also offers a dry run of a motion on
the robot alone, without the table and objects, that does not advance the episode, and helpers
for rotation arithmetic and for lifting a pixel with its depth to a world position. Table 16: DexJoCo tasks, rand-obj setting. All cameras are RGB-D. ∗ Including 30

task definition: the official instruction, the robot and its cameras, the step limit and
wall-clock limit of an episode, the fact that object placement and table height change between
episodes, and the binary success criterion without partial credit. It contains no detail of the
success check. The agent also receives three images of the completed task, the final frames of
three successful human demonstrations from the official dataset, each in a different layout,
taken from the task’s overview camera. They contain no actions and no states. The three
demonstrations are the first that replay successfully in the official environment from initial
layouts that differ from all 150 evaluation layouts. Exploration. A session gives one agent, Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 at the xhigh
effort setting and without subagents, one simulated environment, and a budget of exploration episodes. The agent starts from an empty workspace ( Section 10.1 ). A exploration episode is either interactive, in
which the agent drives the robot itself through the interface or through its own scripts, or a
rehearsal, in which its registered program runs alone in a fresh container on a fresh episode
while the agent is paused; both consume the budget. The agent ends exploration by submitting a
program that has completed a rehearsal without error, so the budget is an upper bound, and over
the 55 sessions the agents used 592 of the 682 budgeted episodes ( Table 17 ).

limited to the official number of steps and to 600 s plus 5 s per allowed step of wall-clock

model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, with a budget of $10 per session. Evaluation. Once the agent has exited, its workspace is frozen, and the submitted
program is evaluated alone on 150 episodes that reproduce the official evaluation of DexJoCo
layout for layout: seeds 0, 1, and 2, with 50 consecutive resets each. Every episode runs in a
fresh simulator process and a fresh container for the program, four episodes in parallel, under
the official step limit and success check, and an error or an early exit of the program counts

We use the 40 tasks of the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites of
LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023 ) in the official environment, numbered from 0 in the official order,
and the position-perturbed variants of LIBERO-Pro ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) , in which objects
exchange their initial regions. The agent commands the official action at 20 Hz, increments of
the end-effector position and rotation, scaled so that 1 corresponds to 5 cm or 0.5 rad, and a
gripper command, with between 1 and 100 rows per call and no motion planner. It observes the scene
through the agent-view and wrist cameras at 256 256 in RGB-D, with the intrinsics and the
pose of each camera, together with the end-effector pose and the finger positions, and it may lift
pixels to 3D points. The step limit of an episode is 220 on Spatial, 280 on Object, 300 on Goal, and
520 on Long, after ten initial steps without motion. Task information. The task description contains the official instruction, the robot
and its cameras, the limits of an episode, and the binary success criterion without the detail of
its check. The agent also receives three images of the
completed task, the final agent-view frames of three official human demonstrations that replay
successfully. Exploration and evaluation. We use Claude Opus 5.5 as our agent in two environments. The budget is
up to 20 exploration episodes on the official placement distribution, followed by at most 5 optional
episodes of adaptation on the perturbed distribution, which the agent may use when it judges that
its program will not transfer ( Section 11.4 ). Exploration layout is a fresh
reset of the official environment with seed , a sample of the official placement
distribution and never a stored initial state. An episode is limited to 600 s plus 5 s per allowed
step. The program is evaluated on the 50 official initial states of each task and on the 50
position-perturbed states of LIBERO-Pro, each episode in a fresh container, synchronously, and with
binary success. Adaptation episodes are fresh resets of the perturbed task with seeds from 2000.
